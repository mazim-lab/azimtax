Incorporating Your Durham Region Business: Tax Advantages and Key Considerations

Published on March 31, 2026

If you're a sole proprietor or freelancer in Durham Region whose business is growing, you've probably heard that incorporation can save you money on taxes. It's one of the most common questions we get from clients in Oshawa, Whitby, and Ajax: "Should I incorporate?" The answer, as with most tax questions, is "it depends." But the potential benefits are significant enough that every successful small business owner should understand them.

Here's a practical breakdown of what incorporation means for your taxes, when it makes sense, and what to watch out for.

The Big Tax Advantage: The Small Business Deduction

The single biggest reason Canadian small business owners incorporate is the small business deduction (SBD). In 2026, Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) pay a combined federal and Ontario tax rate of approximately 12.2% on the first $500,000 of active business income. Compare that to the top personal marginal tax rate in Ontario, which exceeds 53%.

That's a massive gap. If your business earns $200,000 in profit and you don't need all of it for personal expenses, incorporating lets you keep significantly more inside the corporation to reinvest, save, or invest — paying only 12.2% instead of your personal rate.

Salary vs. Dividends: Choosing How You Pay Yourself

Once you're incorporated, you have flexibility in how you draw income from your business. The two main options are salary and dividends, and each has distinct tax implications.

Paying Yourself a Salary

  • Creates a deductible expense for the corporation, reducing its taxable income.
  • Generates RRSP contribution room (16% of earned income, up to the annual maximum).
  • Requires payroll remittances (CPP, income tax withholdings) — more administrative work.
  • Counts as "earned income" for CPP purposes, building your retirement pension.

Paying Yourself Dividends

  • No payroll deductions or CPP contributions required.
  • Taxed at a lower personal rate through the dividend tax credit mechanism.
  • Does not create RRSP room.
  • Simpler to administer — no payroll, no T4s for the dividend portion.

Many business owners use a combination of both. A common strategy is to pay yourself enough salary to maximize your RRSP room, and then take additional income as dividends. The right mix depends on your personal tax situation, and it's worth reviewing annually with your accountant.

Tax Deferral: The Real Power of Incorporation

Incorporation doesn't eliminate taxes — it defers them. You pay the low corporate rate now, and when you eventually withdraw money (as salary or dividends), you pay personal tax at that point. The benefit is the time value of money: those deferred dollars can be invested and grow inside the corporation before you need them personally.

This is most powerful when you consistently earn more than you spend. If you're drawing out every dollar your business makes to cover personal expenses, incorporation may not offer much benefit — and the added costs could outweigh the savings.

When Does Incorporation Make Sense?

Incorporation isn't right for everyone. Here are some general guidelines for Durham Region business owners:

Incorporation likely makes sense if:

  • Your business consistently earns more than $80,000–$100,000 in net profit annually.
  • You don't need to withdraw all your business income for personal living expenses.
  • You want to split income with family members (within the rules set by the tax on split income, or TOSI).
  • You want liability protection — a corporation is a separate legal entity.
  • You're planning for retirement and want to build up investments inside the corporation.

Incorporation may not be worth it if:

  • Your business income is modest and you withdraw most of it for personal use.
  • You can't justify the added costs — annual corporate tax filings (T2), legal fees, and ongoing bookkeeping.
  • You have significant personal tax credits or losses that offset your income already.

The Costs to Consider

Incorporating isn't free. In Ontario, you'll pay a provincial incorporation fee (approximately $300 if you file online through ServiceOntario) or slightly more if you incorporate federally through Corporations Canada. Beyond the initial setup, ongoing costs include:

  • Annual corporate tax return (T2): More complex than a personal return, typically costing $2,000+ to prepare professionally.
  • Bookkeeping: Corporations require clean, separate books — no mixing personal and business finances.
  • Payroll administration: If you pay yourself a salary, you'll need to handle payroll remittances to the CRA.
  • Legal maintenance: Annual resolutions, maintaining a minute book, and keeping your corporate registration current.

For a business earning $60,000 a year, these costs could eat into any tax savings. For a business earning $150,000+, the math usually works out strongly in favour of incorporation.

A Note on the Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption

One often-overlooked benefit of incorporation is access to the Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption (LCGE). If your corporation qualifies as a qualifying small business corporation, you may be able to shelter up to approximately $1,016,836 (2026 limit) in capital gains when you sell your shares — completely tax-free. This is a significant planning tool for business owners looking toward an eventual exit or sale.
