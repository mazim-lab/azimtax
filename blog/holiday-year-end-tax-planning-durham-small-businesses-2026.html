Holiday Spending and Year-End Tax Planning for Durham Region Small Businesses: 7 Tax-Smart Moves Before December 31

November is when holiday spending and year-end decisions begin to overlap. A client gift, staff party, new laptop, or charitable donation may have a different tax result depending on what was purchased, why it was purchased, and how it was documented. For small businesses in Oshawa, Whitby, Ajax, Pickering, Clarington, and across Durham Region, now is the time to organize those decisions—not after the books close. These seven steps can help you approach the final weeks of 2026 with cleaner records and fewer surprises.

1. Set a business purpose for client gifts

Before buying client gifts, decide who will receive them and how the expense supports the business relationship. Keep the receipt, recipient list, date, and a short note explaining the business purpose. Avoid recording every holiday purchase as “advertising” without support. Meals, entertainment, gift certificates, branded merchandise, and ordinary gifts can be treated differently for income tax and HST purposes. Clear records allow your tax preparer to classify each item properly.

2. Track staff gifts and awards separately

The CRA has administrative policies for certain non-cash gifts and awards provided to employees, but the details matter. Cash, near-cash items, performance rewards, and long-service awards do not all receive the same treatment. Record the employee's name, the reason for the gift, its fair market value, and whether it was cash, a gift card, or another item. Ask for advice before payroll closes if an amount may be a taxable benefit, since it could affect deductions and year-end slips.

3. Keep holiday party costs well documented

If you hold a staff holiday party, retain invoices for the venue, catering, entertainment, transportation, and related costs. Note who attended and whether the event was generally available to all employees at a particular location. The usual limitation on meals and entertainment has exceptions, including some qualifying staff events, but the conditions and annual-event limits should be reviewed. Expenses involving clients or owners may require separate treatment, so do not combine everything into one unsupported journal entry.

4. Review equipment purchases before December 31

Do you genuinely need a computer, tool, vehicle, office fixture, or other long-term asset? Review the business need, cash flow, delivery date, and tax treatment before ordering. Buying something solely to create a deduction rarely makes financial sense. Capital assets are generally deducted over time rather than expensed immediately, although available incentives may change the timing. The asset normally must be available for use, not merely ordered, before year-end treatment applies. Keep the invoice, payment record, serial number, and date it entered service.

5. Count inventory and identify obsolete stock

Retailers, trades, restaurants, and product-based businesses should plan a physical inventory count close to year-end. Reconcile quantities to the bookkeeping system and document damaged, expired, slow-moving, or obsolete items. Inventory valuation affects cost of goods sold and taxable income, so an estimate without support can distort the return. Do not simply write down stock because it has not sold recently; retain evidence of condition, selling prices, and the method used to value it.

6. Plan charitable giving carefully

If the business intends to support a charity during the holiday season, confirm that the organization is a registered charity and obtain an official donation receipt. A sponsorship that provides advertising or promotional benefits may be a business expense rather than a charitable gift. The payer also matters: a donation made personally by an owner is not automatically a corporate donation. Decide who is giving, document any benefit received, and avoid assuming that a crowdfunding contribution will qualify for a tax credit or deduction.

7. Prepare a November year-end package

Create one folder for bank and credit card statements, loan balances, payroll reports, HST filings, major purchase invoices, vehicle logs, home-office details, and shareholder transactions. Add a list of unpaid customer invoices and supplier bills. Then compare year-to-date results with 2025 and your 2026 budget. Unusual balances, duplicate expenses, negative asset accounts, and uncategorized transactions are easier to investigate while the details are fresh.

Turn holiday activity into reliable records

Good year-end planning is less about finding a last-minute write-off and more about making sound business decisions with complete documentation. Separate client, employee, and owner expenses; confirm the treatment of unusual items; and give your bookkeeper time to reconcile the accounts before December 31. A short review in November can make tax preparation faster and help management start 2027 with more useful financial information.
