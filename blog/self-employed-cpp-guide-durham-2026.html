The Self-Employed Guide to CPP: How it Works and Why it Matters for Your Durham Business

Published on March 9, 2026

As a self-employed individual in Durham Region, you wear many hats: CEO, lead salesperson, and chief coffee-maker. You also take on the role of both employer and employee, a distinction that becomes critically important when it comes to the Canada Pension Plan (CPP). Unlike traditional employees who have CPP contributions automatically deducted from their paycheques, you are responsible for paying the entire amount yourself.

Understanding how CPP works is essential for managing your cash flow, meeting your CRA obligations, and planning for your future. Let's break down what freelancers, contractors, and small business owners in Ajax, Whitby, and Oshawa need to know.

1. You Pay Both Halves: The "Double" Contribution

For salaried employees, their employer matches their CPP contributions. When you're self-employed, you are responsible for paying both the employee *and* the employer portions. This means that while an employee contributed 5.95% of their pensionable earnings in 2025, a self-employed person contributed the full 11.9%.

This can come as a surprise when you file your taxes for the first time. The full amount is calculated on your self-employment income and becomes part of your total tax payable. Setting aside funds throughout the year is crucial to avoid a large, unexpected bill.

2. How is Your CPP Contribution Calculated?

Your CPP contribution is based on your net self-employment income (your business revenue minus your business expenses). The calculation works as follows:

  • Basic Exemption: You don't pay CPP on the first $3,500 of your income.
  • Pensionable Earnings: You contribute on your income between the $3,500 basic exemption and the "Yearly Maximum Pensionable Earnings" (YMPE), which is $68,500 for 2025.
  • The Calculation: (Your Net Self-Employment Income - $3,500) x 11.9% = Your CPP Contribution.

If your net income is below $3,500, you don't have to contribute to CPP. If it's above the YMPE, you only contribute up to that maximum limit.

3. The Tax Deduction vs. The Tax Credit

While paying both halves might seem daunting, the CRA provides some tax relief. The CPP contribution is split into two parts for tax purposes:

  • The Employee Portion (5.95%): This part is claimed as a non-refundable tax credit. It reduces your federal and provincial tax payable directly.
  • The Employer Portion (5.95%): This part is claimed as a business expense. It reduces your net income, which in turn lowers the amount of income you pay tax on.

Good bookkeeping software or a professional accountant can ensure you are claiming both of these correctly to maximize your tax savings.

4. Planning for CPP Payments

The best strategy to manage your CPP obligations is to plan ahead. Instead of being hit with a large bill at tax time, consider the following:

  • Tax Instalments: If your net tax owing is more than $3,000 in two of the last three years, the CRA will require you to pay your taxes in quarterly instalments. Your CPP contribution is factored into this amount.
  • Set Aside Funds: A good rule of thumb is to set aside 25-30% of every payment you receive into a separate savings account. This will cover your income tax, HST (if applicable), and your CPP contributions.

Conclusion

For Durham Region's entrepreneurs, the Canada Pension Plan is a mandatory part of doing business. It's your retirement safety net, and managing it properly is a sign of a healthy, sustainable business. By understanding your obligations and planning throughout the year, you can avoid tax-time stress and focus on what you do best: growing your business.
