Fall Tax Planning for Durham Region Small Businesses: 7 Moves to Make Before Year-End 2026

September gives Durham Region business owners something year-end does not: time. With several months left in 2026, you can still correct bookkeeping gaps, prepare for tax payments, and make informed business decisions before the calendar closes. Waiting until filing season usually means fewer choices and more rushed paperwork.

Whether you operate in Oshawa, Whitby, Ajax, Pickering, or Clarington, fall is an ideal season for a practical tax checkup. The goal is not to spend money simply to claim deductions. It is to understand where your business stands, document legitimate expenses, and avoid preventable surprises. Start with these seven steps.

1. Update your books through the end of summer

Tax planning is only as useful as the numbers behind it. Reconcile every business bank and credit card account, record outstanding invoices, and identify transactions that still need receipts or explanations. Review accounts receivable and follow up on overdue customer balances. Clean books give you a reliable year-to-date profit figure, which is the foundation for estimating income tax, planning cash flow, and making sensible decisions before December.

2. Compare 2026 profit with last year and your forecast

Once your records are current, compare year-to-date revenue, gross margin, and operating expenses with the same period in 2025. Then prepare a realistic forecast for the rest of 2026. If profit is higher than expected, you may need to reserve more cash for corporate or personal tax. If results are weaker, the forecast can reveal whether expenses need attention or whether upcoming purchases should be delayed. A forecast is not a tax return, but it gives you time to act.

3. Review CRA instalments and upcoming payment obligations

Some individuals and corporations must make income tax instalments during the year. Compare amounts already paid with your CRA notices and your current profit estimate. Do not assume last year's payment pattern automatically fits this year. Also list upcoming HST and payroll remittances so those funds are not mistaken for available operating cash. Missing or underpaying required amounts can result in interest, so confirm your specific obligations before changing an instalment.

4. Plan necessary equipment purchases carefully

If your business genuinely needs a computer, tool, vehicle, or other long-term asset, consider the timing before year-end. Most capital purchases are not deducted in the same way as ordinary supplies; they are generally claimed over time under capital cost allowance rules. The available deduction can depend on the asset, when it becomes available for use, and the rules in effect. Get advice before buying a major asset for tax reasons, and keep the invoice, payment record, and business-use details.

5. Capture legitimate expenses and separate personal costs

Review recurring costs such as software, insurance, professional fees, advertising, telephone, and home-office expenses. Make sure legitimate business purchases are recorded and supported, but remove personal spending from expense accounts. For mixed-use costs such as a vehicle or mobile phone, maintain a reasonable business-use calculation and the records behind it. Good documentation supports your deductions and makes a CRA review easier; a bank statement alone may not explain what was purchased or why.

6. Check HST and payroll before the fourth quarter

Compare HST collected and input tax credits with filed returns and your general ledger. Look for expenses where HST was claimed without adequate support or where sales tax was coded incorrectly. For payroll, reconcile wages, deductions, and CRA remittances, then identify taxable benefits or bonuses that may require year-end treatment. Fixing these items in September is much easier than rebuilding twelve months of records while preparing T4 slips or an annual return.

7. Schedule an owner-compensation and year-end meeting

Incorporated owner-managers should review salary, dividends, shareholder transactions, and cash needs with a tax professional before making final decisions. The right approach depends on personal income, corporate profit, retirement goals, and other factors; there is no universal answer. Unincorporated owners can use the same meeting to review projected taxable income and deductible costs. Bring current financial statements, prior-year returns, CRA notices, and details of any major 2026 changes.

Turn fall planning into a cleaner year-end

A useful fall tax review produces a short action list: missing records to collect, accounts to reconcile, payments to reserve for, and decisions that need professional advice. It should also leave your bookkeeping current enough to monitor the final months of the year. That preparation can reduce filing-season stress and help you make business decisions based on facts rather than a rough bank balance.
