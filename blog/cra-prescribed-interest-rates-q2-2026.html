CRA Prescribed Interest Rates Q2 2026: Why You Can't Afford to File Late

The Canada Revenue Agency has just confirmed the interest rates for the second quarter of 2026. If you owe money to the taxman, procrastination is an expensive habit you'll want to break immediately.

It’s a common scenario: tax season arrives, cash flow is tight, and you decide to file a little late or hold off on paying your balance owing. In previous years, when interest rates were historically low, this might have felt like a cheap loan from the government. That is no longer the case.

The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) has announced the prescribed interest rates for the second quarter of 2026 (April 1, 2026 – June 30, 2026). The headline? It’s still expensive to owe the CRA money.

The Numbers You Need to Know

For Q2 2026, the prescribed interest rates are holding steady at high levels:

  • 7% on overdue taxes, Canada Pension Plan (CPP) contributions, and Employment Insurance (EI) premiums.
  • 3% on prescribed rate loans (often used for income splitting with family members).
  • 5% on overpaid taxes (interest the CRA pays you—corporate taxpayers typically get a lower rate).

While seeing the rate hold at 7% is better than an increase, it remains significantly higher than the rates we saw just a few years ago. For small business owners in Durham Region, this 7% interest is not tax-deductible, making the effective cost of this debt much higher than a business line of credit.

Why "Treating the CRA Like a Bank" is a Bad Idea

Some business owners treat their tax debt like a revolving line of credit. When cash is tight, they prioritize suppliers and payroll, leaving the CRA for last. With the rate at 7%, this is a dangerous strategy for three reasons:

  1. Non-Deductible Interest: Unlike interest on a business loan, which is a tax write-off, interest paid to the CRA comes out of your after-tax profit. To pay $1,000 in CRA interest, your business might need to earn $1,200 or $1,300 in revenue depending on your tax bracket.
  2. Compounding Daily: The CRA compounds interest daily. A debt left unpaid for months can balloon surprisingly fast.
  3. Risk of Penalties: Interest is just the start. If you file late, you’re hit with a late-filing penalty (5% of the balance owing + 1% for each full month late). If you’re a repeat offender, those penalties can double.

What If You Can't Pay?

We see this often with clients in Oshawa and Whitby—you’ve had a tough year, and you simply don’t have the cash to pay the full tax bill by April 30th (or June 15th for self-employed individuals). Do not delay filing just because you can't pay.

Here is the golden rule of tax trouble: File on time, pay what you can.

By filing on time, you avoid the late-filing penalty (the 5% hit). You will still be charged the 7% interest on the balance owing, but you avoid the punitive fines. Once you’ve filed, you can contact the CRA to arrange a payment plan. They are generally willing to work with taxpayers who are communicative and compliant with filing, rather than those who hide and ignore deadlines.

Prescribed Rate Loans: Still a Viable Strategy?

On a lighter note, the prescribed rate for family loans remains at 3%. This rate is used for "income splitting" strategies where a higher-income earner loans money to a lower-income spouse or family trust to invest. The investment income is then taxed in the hands of the lower-income family member.

With the rate at 3%, this strategy is less lucrative than when it was 1%, but it can still offer tax savings if the investment returns exceed 3%. If you have significant investment capital, it’s worth discussing with an accountant to see if the math still works for your family.

The Bottom Line for 2026

As we approach the April 30th deadline, the message from the CRA’s Q2 rates is clear: prioritize your tax payments. If you are struggling with cash flow, look into a bank loan or line of credit first—the interest is often lower than 7%, and unlike CRA interest, it may be tax-deductible if used for business purposes.
