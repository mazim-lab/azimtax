Seasonal Tax Planning: A Spring Checklist for Durham Region Business Owners

Most business owners think about taxes once a year — in April, when it's already too late to do much about them. A quick spring review can change that entirely.

Tax planning shouldn't be a December scramble or an April panic. For small business owners in Oshawa, Whitby, Ajax, and across Durham Region, spring is actually the best time to step back and look at where you stand — before the year gets away from you.

Here's a practical checklist you can work through right now, whether you run a landscaping company in Clarington or a consulting practice in Pickering.

1. Review Last Year's Return Before You Forget It

Your 2025 tax return is either freshly filed or about to be. Before you toss it in a drawer, spend 15 minutes reviewing it with one question in mind: where did the money go?

Look at your biggest expense categories. Were there deductions you missed? Did your effective tax rate surprise you? If your accountant prepared the return, now is the time to ask questions — not next February when the details are fuzzy.

Common items people miss on their first pass:

  • Vehicle expenses (if you use your car for business)
  • Home office deductions (especially post-pandemic)
  • Professional development and training costs
  • Meals and entertainment (50% deductible for business purposes)

2. Set Up Your Bookkeeping for the Rest of the Year

If your books from January and February are already behind, fix that now. The longer you wait, the more painful catch-up becomes. This isn't just about compliance — clean books give you visibility into cash flow, profitability, and where you're actually spending money.

If you're still using spreadsheets or shoeboxes, consider moving to cloud accounting software. QuickBooks Online and Wave are both popular with Durham Region small businesses. The investment pays for itself in time saved and errors avoided.

3. Check Your HST Situation

If your business revenue is approaching or has crossed the $30,000 threshold, you need to register for HST — and you may already be late. The CRA requires registration within 29 days of the supply that pushed you over the limit.

Already registered? Review your Input Tax Credits (ITCs). Many business owners leave money on the table by not claiming HST paid on legitimate business expenses. Keep those receipts organized and make sure your bookkeeper is capturing them.

4. Think About Your Business Structure

Spring is a good time to ask: am I operating under the right structure? If you're a sole proprietor earning over $60,000–$80,000 in net business income, incorporating might save you significant tax through the small business deduction.

The small business tax rate in Ontario is approximately 12.2% on the first $500,000 of active business income, compared to personal marginal rates that can exceed 50% at higher income levels. The math doesn't always favour incorporation (there are costs and complexity involved), but it's worth running the numbers.

5. Plan for Quarterly Instalments

If you owed more than $3,000 in net tax last year (or in either of the two preceding years), the CRA expects you to make quarterly instalment payments. Missing these results in instalment interest charges.

The next instalment deadlines for 2026:

  • March 15 — Q1 instalment
  • June 15 — Q2 instalment
  • September 15 — Q3 instalment
  • December 15 — Q4 instalment

Set calendar reminders now. The CRA doesn't send you a bill — they expect you to calculate and remit on your own.

6. Maximize RRSP and TFSA Contributions

If you're a sole proprietor, your RRSP room is based on your net self-employment income from last year. Contributing early in the year means your investments have more time to grow tax-sheltered.

Don't overlook the TFSA either. While contributions aren't tax-deductible, all investment growth inside a TFSA is completely tax-free — permanently. For 2026, the annual TFSA limit is $7,000, and if you've never contributed, your cumulative room could be over $95,000.

7. Document Everything — Starting Now

The CRA can audit you for up to six years. If you can't produce receipts and records to support your deductions, you lose them. Period.

Set up a simple system: photograph receipts with your phone, save them to a dedicated folder (Google Drive, Dropbox, whatever works), and categorize them monthly. Your future self — and your accountant — will thank you.

Spring Cleaning for Your Finances

Tax planning doesn't have to be complicated. A few hours of review in the spring can save you thousands at year-end and eliminate the stress of last-minute scrambles. The businesses that pay the least tax aren't doing anything exotic — they're just organized and proactive.

If you're not sure where to start, or if your situation involves complexities like rental income, foreign assets, or multiple business ventures, a quick conversation with an accountant can point you in the right direction.
