Tax Considerations for Canadians Working in Dubai and the UAE

Published on March 5, 2026

The United Arab Emirates, particularly Dubai, has long been a magnet for Canadian professionals seeking career opportunities and a tax-free lifestyle. While the UAE does not levy personal income tax, this doesn't automatically mean you are free from your obligations to the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA). Understanding your Canadian tax residency status is the critical first step for any Canadian moving to or living in the UAE.

At Azim Tax & Accounting, we specialize in expatriate tax services for clients in Durham Region and beyond. Here’s what every Canadian in the UAE needs to know about their taxes back home.

1. Determining Your Tax Residency Status

Your entire Canadian tax obligation hinges on your residency status. You can be a resident, a non-resident, a factual resident, or a deemed resident. For the purpose of moving to the UAE, the goal for most is to become a non-resident for tax purposes.

To be considered a non-resident, you must sever your significant residential ties to Canada. These include:

  • A home in Canada: Selling or renting out your Canadian property to an arm's-length party is a primary tie.
  • A spouse or common-law partner in Canada: If your spouse remains in Canada, the CRA will almost always consider you a resident.
  • Dependants in Canada: Similar to a spouse, if your children or other dependants remain in Canada, it's a strong tie.

Secondary ties are also considered, such as personal property (cars, furniture), social ties (club memberships), and economic ties (bank accounts, credit cards). The more ties you sever, the stronger your case for non-residency becomes.

2. The Importance of Form NR73 (Determination of Residency Status)

Before you leave Canada, or shortly after, it is highly advisable to file Form NR73, Determination of Residency Status (Leaving Canada). This form asks detailed questions about the ties you are severing and establishing abroad. The CRA will review it and issue an opinion on your residency status.

While the CRA's opinion is not legally binding, it provides a significant level of certainty. If they agree you are a non-resident, you can proceed with confidence. If they don’t, it gives you a chance to understand their reasoning and potentially sever more ties.

3. Filing Obligations as a Non-Resident

Even as a non-resident, you may still have filing obligations in Canada. If you have Canadian-sourced income, you must file a Canadian tax return. Common examples include:

  • Rental income from Canadian property: You'll be subject to a 25% withholding tax on the gross rental income, which your agent must remit. You can also elect to file a Section 216 return to be taxed on the net rental income.
  • Pensions, dividends, or other investment income: This income is typically subject to Part XIII withholding tax.
  • Capital gains from the sale of Canadian property: You must report the disposition of taxable Canadian property.

If you have no Canadian-sourced income, you generally do not have to file a Canadian tax return once you are a non-resident.

4. The Canada-UAE Tax Treaty

Canada has a tax treaty with the UAE to prevent double taxation. While the UAE doesn't have a personal income tax, the treaty is still crucial. It contains a "tie-breaker" rule that helps determine residency if both countries claim you as a resident. This usually comes down to where you have a "permanent home available" and where your "centre of vital interests" lies.

Conclusion

Moving to the UAE offers incredible opportunities, but it requires careful tax planning. Simply leaving Canada is not enough to sever your tax obligations. You must proactively demonstrate your intent to become a non-resident by cutting significant residential ties. Failing to do so can result in the CRA taxing your worldwide income, including your UAE earnings, which can be a costly mistake.
