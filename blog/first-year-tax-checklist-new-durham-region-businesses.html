First-Year Tax Checklist for New Durham Region Businesses: 8 Steps Before Your First Filing

Starting a business in Oshawa, Whitby, Ajax, Pickering, Clarington, or elsewhere in Durham Region comes with plenty of decisions. Tax administration is easy to postpone when you are focused on customers and cash flow, but the habits you establish in year one can determine whether your first filing is orderly or expensive to clean up. Use this checklist to build a practical tax system from the beginning.

1. Confirm your business structure

A sole proprietorship and a corporation do not file tax the same way. A sole proprietor generally reports business income and expenses with a personal income tax return, while a corporation files its own T2 corporate return. Incorporation also creates separate legal records, owner-pay decisions, and filing obligations. Do not treat money moving between you and a corporation as an informal withdrawal. Record salary, dividends, reimbursements, and shareholder transactions properly.

2. Separate business and personal transactions

Open a dedicated business bank account and use a separate credit card where practical. Separation does not make an expense deductible, but it creates a cleaner audit trail and reduces missed or duplicated entries. If you pay a business cost personally, keep the receipt and record the reimbursement or owner contribution. Personal purchases paid by the business should also be identified promptly instead of being left in an expense category.

3. Build a receipt system before volume grows

Save invoices, receipts, contracts, mileage records, and proof of payment as transactions occur. A bank statement shows that money moved; it may not show what was purchased, the business purpose, or whether HST was charged. Choose one digital storage system, use consistent filenames, and connect each document to the matching bookkeeping entry. Keep records for the retention period that applies to your situation, generally at least six years after the end of the relevant tax year.

4. Understand when HST registration applies

Many businesses must register once they stop qualifying as a small supplier, generally when worldwide taxable revenues exceed $30,000 under the applicable test. The timing rules can differ depending on whether the threshold is exceeded in one calendar quarter or over several consecutive quarters. Voluntary registration may make sense earlier for some businesses, but it also brings charging, filing, payment, and recordkeeping responsibilities. Review the rules before you cross the threshold—not after invoices have already gone out.

5. Track startup costs and equipment separately

Keep records for costs incurred while preparing to open, including registration fees, professional advice, advertising, software, supplies, and insurance. The tax treatment depends on what was purchased and when the business began. Long-term assets such as computers, tools, furniture, and vehicles are usually not handled like ordinary operating expenses; they may fall under capital cost allowance rules. Record the purchase date, tax, total cost, and date the asset became available for use.

6. Set up payroll before the first payday

If you hire employees, establish the payroll account and deduction process before paying wages. Obtain the required employee information, calculate income tax, CPP, and EI correctly, track employer contributions and vacation pay, and remit on your assigned schedule. Worker status matters too. Calling someone a contractor does not make it so if the actual relationship has the characteristics of employment. Misclassification can lead to assessments for deductions, employer amounts, interest, and penalties.

7. Forecast income tax and CPP

Revenue is not the same as taxable profit, and the balance in your bank account is not your tax bill. Update your books monthly and estimate net business income after supportable expenses. Self-employed individuals may owe both income tax and CPP contributions, while corporations have their own tax payments and owner-compensation considerations. Set aside funds regularly and keep HST collected separate from operating cash. CRA instalments may be required in future periods based on taxes owing.

8. Put every deadline on one calendar

Your calendar may include HST returns and payments, payroll remittances, information slips, personal or corporate tax filings, and tax balances due. Filing and payment dates are not always the same. Your first corporate year-end may also be different from the calendar year. Create reminders well in advance and schedule a bookkeeping review before each deadline so missing documents can be resolved without a last-minute scramble.

Make the first year easier to file

A strong first-year system is simple: separate accounts, current books, complete documents, and a calendar matched to your registrations. Review these items monthly and ask for advice before major changes such as incorporating, hiring, registering for HST, buying a vehicle, or taking money from a corporation. Early guidance is usually less costly than correcting a year of inconsistent records.
