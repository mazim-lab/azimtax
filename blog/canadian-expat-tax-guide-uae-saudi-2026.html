Essential Tax Guide for Canadian Expats in the UAE and Saudi Arabia

Published on February 21, 2026

For many Canadians, the allure of tax-free salaries in the Middle East—specifically in hubs like Dubai, Abu Dhabi, and Riyadh—is hard to resist. Whether you're an engineer in the oil sector, a healthcare professional, or a teacher, moving to the UAE or Saudi Arabia can be a lucrative career step. However, leaving Canada doesn't automatically mean leaving the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) behind. Understanding your tax obligations is critical to avoiding costly penalties and double taxation.

Residency: The Foundation of Expat Taxation

The single most important factor in your tax situation is your residency status. The CRA determines this based on your "residential ties" to Canada, not just your physical location.

  • Factual Resident: If you keep significant ties—like a home available for your use, a spouse or dependents in Canada, or personal property (cars, furniture)—you may still be considered a resident for tax purposes. This means you must report your worldwide income to the CRA, including that "tax-free" salary earned in the Gulf.
  • Non-Resident: To be considered a non-resident, you generally must sever most residential ties. This often involves selling or long-term leasing your home, moving your spouse and dependents with you, and canceling provincial health coverage. As a non-resident, you are only taxed on Canadian-sourced income.

Tax Obligations for Non-Residents

If you successfully establish non-resident status, your tax life becomes simpler, but not non-existent. You are no longer taxed on your UAE or Saudi employment income in Canada. However, you must still file a Canadian tax return if you have:

  • Rental Income: If you rent out your Canadian home, you must remit 25% of the gross rent to the CRA each month as a non-resident withholding tax. You can elect to file a Section 216 return to pay tax on net rental income (profit) instead, often resulting in a refund.
  • Investments: Dividends and certain other investment income from Canadian sources are subject to withholding tax (usually 25%, unless a tax treaty reduces it). Interest income is typically exempt for non-residents.
  • Disposing of Property: Selling Canadian real estate as a non-resident triggers complex compliance rules (Certificate of Compliance) to ensure capital gains tax is paid.

The Section 217 Election

For some non-residents receiving Canadian pensions or other benefits, the Section 217 election can be beneficial. This allows you to file a Canadian tax return reporting your worldwide income to determine if the tax rate would be lower than the standard non-resident withholding tax. It's complex, but for retirees in the Gulf, it can lead to significant savings.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

We often see expats make avoidable mistakes:

  1. Failing to file a departure return: The year you leave, you must file a final return indicating your date of departure. This is when "deemed disposition" (departure tax) on certain assets may apply.
  2. Keeping provincial health cards: Holding onto OHIP or other provincial coverage is a strong indicator of residential ties. Cancel it when you leave permanently.
  3. Ignoring the NR4 slip: Ensure your Canadian financial institutions know you are a non-resident so they issue the correct slips (NR4) and withhold the correct tax.
